Backtesting and variable tuning — what happened, what we learned, and what comes next
The backtesting journey
The backtesting we ran today was designed to answer one specific question: does using intraday Visual Crossing observations to adjust the probability model actually improve the quality of our temperature bin predictions? We couldn't test everything at once because certain data doesn't exist historically (we have no archive of "what did our hourly forecast path look like on March 15 at 2pm?"), so we deliberately split the problem into two parts. Part 1, which we completed today, tests only the observed-max floor — the rule that says "the daily high can't be less than what's already been observed, so zero out probability mass below that point and redistribute." Part 2, which tests the intraday residual (the "running hotter or colder than expected" signal), requires either proper archived forecast hourly paths or several weeks of live shadow-mode data, so it's deferred until that data accumulates.

The backtest infrastructure lives in a separate database file (bot/data/backtest.db) to keep experimental data completely isolated from live trading state. We backfilled 108 resolved events across the last few days using free hourly temperature data from the Open-Meteo Archive API (ERA5 reanalysis), and derived empirical forecast uncertainty (σ) from the 160,000+ forecast error records already stored in forecast_errors. The replay script simulates every 20 minutes from 6 AM to 8 PM UTC for each event, computes what the model would have predicted under different configurations, and scores each prediction against the actual observed daily high using the Brier score (a standard probability calibration metric where lower is better).

What the backtesting revealed
The first run tested the floor alone — truncating the probability distribution below the observed max without changing σ. The result was surprising: the floor made calibration worse, not better. Brier score went from 0.0902 (baseline) to 0.0914 (floor only). The degradation got progressively worse through the day, with the 5-8 PM window showing the largest harm. The diagnosis was straightforward: the floor removes probability mass from below but leaves σ at the full-day value of ~2.1°C, so the remaining distribution is squeezed into fewer bins with an unrealistically wide spread. The model becomes overconfident on the wrong bins. Predicted probabilities of 0.98 were only empirically correct 35% of the time — classic overconfidence from an internally inconsistent model (correct constraint + incorrect uncertainty).

This led directly to the σ-shrinkage implementation. The idea is that as the day progresses, the remaining uncertainty about the daily max naturally decreases — if it's 3 PM and we already know the temperature path, there's less room for surprise than at 6 AM. We implemented a two-factor σ model: a time factor based on remaining daylight fraction (using the astral library for proper sunrise/sunset at each latitude), and a residual factor that widens σ back up on days that are behaving unexpectedly (preventing the model from becoming falsely confident on weird days). The shrunk σ has two hard floors to prevent dangerous collapse: an absolute minimum of 1.5°C and a relative minimum of 35% of the original blended σ.

We ran the backtest across four variants simultaneously — baseline (no floor, no shrinkage), floor-only (the failed v1), v2_time (floor + time-based σ shrinkage, which is the primary production candidate), and v2_time_surprise (floor + time + day-level surprise widening, which is exploratory). The v2_time variant produced a 15.1% Brier improvement over baseline (0.0902 → 0.0766), with consistent improvement across every hour bucket including the critical 5-8 PM window. The number of dangerously overconfident predictions (predicted ≥0.7 that turned out wrong) dropped from 841 in floor-only to just 37 in v2_time — a 96% reduction. The maximum predicted probability was 0.748, just barely crossing the 0.7 threshold, which means the model naturally self-limits at the tail without needing artificial flattening.

We also tested probability tail flattening (raising each bin probability to the power of 0.95 and renormalizing) as a way to further tame overconfident tails. This backfired: it made Brier 7.6% worse because it can't help degenerate distributions where all mass is on a single bin (which happens when the floor eliminates all but one bin), and it hurts the well-calibrated mid-range predictions that make up the bulk of the data. Flattening was disabled (exponent set to 1.0) based on this evidence.

The variables in .env and what they control
The .env file now contains three groups of live-adjustment variables, all of which were tuned through the backtesting process.

The v1 residual adjustment group controls how the intraday temperature residual (current temp minus expected temp at this hour) adjusts the forecast mean (μ). USE_LIVE_ADJUSTMENT=true is the master switch — when false, the bot trades on the pure ECMWF+GFS+climatology blend. LIVE_ADJ_ALPHA=0.5 is the damping factor on the smoothed residual; it means only half the observed residual gets applied to the forecast. LIVE_ADJ_TOD_PEAK_HOUR=13 and LIVE_ADJ_TOD_RAMP_HOURS=5 define a triangular time-of-day weighting that peaks at 1 PM local time (residuals at noon matter more than residuals at dawn). LIVE_ADJ_MIN_OBS=2 requires at least two observations before trusting the residual (cold-start protection). LIVE_ADJ_MAX_ABS_C=3.0 and LIVE_ADJ_MAX_SIGMA=2.0 are hard caps — the μ adjustment can never exceed 3°C or 2× the blended σ, whichever is smaller. LIVE_ADJ_OBS_FLOOR=true enables the observed-max truncation on bin probabilities. LIVE_ADJ_SMOOTH_WINDOW_MIN=60 sets the residual smoothing window to 60 minutes (averaging the last ~3 observations to reduce single-station noise).

The v2 σ-shrinkage group was the primary output of today's backtesting. LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_SHRINK=true enables dynamic σ shrinkage in production — this was the final flag we flipped after the backtest passed all four gating criteria. LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_TIME_MIN=0.50 sets the floor on the time factor at 0.50 (meaning σ can shrink to at most half of its original value from time-of-day alone, before the absolute floor kicks in). In practice the absolute floor dominates: LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_ABS_FLOOR_C=1.5 means σ never goes below 1.5°C regardless of how late in the day it is. LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_REL_FLOOR=0.35 is a second safety net — σ never drops below 35% of the blended σ. LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_RESIDUAL_DIV=3.0 and LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_RESIDUAL_CAP=0.5 control the residual widening factor: on days where the temperature is running far from expectations, σ widens by up to 50% (capped) to prevent overconfidence. LIVE_ADJ_TAIL_FLATTEN_EXPONENT=1.0 disables the tail flattening that we tested and found harmful.

The VC diagnostic group controls the shadow measurement layer. VC_DIAG_DISAGREE_LARGE_C=2 flags events where VC's projected daily high disagrees with our blended μ by 2°C or more. VC_DIAG_WARN_DIRECTIONAL_C=1.5 flags events where VC predicts meaningfully hotter or colder. VC_DIAG_FUTURE_DAY_HORIZON=1 means we pull VC forecasts for D+1 events (tomorrow) in addition to same-day. VC_DIAG_RMSE_WINDOW_HOURS=6 computes the hourly-path RMSE over the next 6 hours only (where both our model and VC have high confidence). VC_DIAG_BIN_WIDTH_C=0.556 is the approximate bin width in Celsius (1°F) used to translate disagreements into a "bins apart" count.

What additional data the bot now collects as it runs
Every 20 minutes, for each active event, the bot now writes: a live_observations row (current temperature, humidity, cloud cover, wind, precip, conditions, observed max so far, station list), and a vc_forecast_diagnostics row (VC's projected daily high, disagreement with our model in °C and bins, hourly-path RMSE, and warning flags). Every 2 hours, the bot writes forecast_runs rows (one per source per event, capturing the ECMWF and GFS μ/σ at that pull time) and forecast_hourly rows (up to 72 hourly data points per run). Also every 2 hours at the :07 mark, the bot fetches VC forecasts for D+1 events and writes future-day diagnostic rows. Every trading cycle at :10, decision_snapshots records the complete context for every bin evaluated — both the blend and the adjustment state, plus the VC diagnostic at that moment.

All of this is append-only. Historical rows are never overwritten. A nightly retention job at 04:30 UTC purges forecast_hourly, live_observations, and vc_forecast_diagnostics rows older than 90 days. Summary tables (forecast_runs, decision_snapshots, positions, temp_events, temp_outcomes) are kept indefinitely.

What future backtesting we can do as data accumulates
The most important future backtest is Part 2: residual validation. Right now, every decision_snapshots row stores both blended_mu_c and adjusted_mu_c, plus model_prob_blended and model_prob (the active adjusted probability) on temp_outcomes. After 2-4 weeks of live data, we can join these against the actual daily max (from historical_observed_daily, which the bias updater populates automatically) and compute Brier scores for blended vs adjusted — but this time with the real residual driving the adjustment, not a proxy. If the adjusted probabilities have lower Brier than blended, the residual is doing its job. If they don't, we should reduce LIVE_ADJ_ALPHA or tighten the caps.

We can also do a parameter sweep on the accumulated data. The backtest script (bot/scripts/adjustment_backtest.py) already supports --variant flags, and the cached data in backtest.db means re-running with different parameter values is free and takes about 10 seconds. As the event corpus grows (we had 108 events today; in a month we'll have thousands), the statistical power of these sweeps increases dramatically. Specific parameters worth sweeping: LIVE_ADJ_ALPHA (the current 0.5 is a guess), LIVE_ADJ_SIGMA_ABS_FLOOR_C (1.5 worked but 1.3 or 1.7 might be better with more data), and LIVE_ADJ_TOD_PEAK_HOUR (13 is a heuristic; different climates peak at different hours).

The VC diagnostic layer enables a separate line of future analysis. After 2+ weeks, run python bot/scripts/vc_diagnostic_report.py --days 14 to see whether VC disagreement has predictive value. If it does, the natural next steps (in order of increasing complexity) are: a risk-layer filter that blocks or sizes down trades with large VC disagreement, a confidence penalty (halve Kelly size when VC disagrees), or incorporating VC as a third forecast source in the blend.

Finally, the forecast_runs time series enables forecast drift analysis — do events where ECMWF shifted significantly between pulls tend to resolve differently than events where the forecast was stable? The forecast_delta_mu_c stored on temp_events and decision_snapshots already captures this; a simple query grouping closed positions by delta magnitude would reveal whether forecast instability predicts worse outcomes.
